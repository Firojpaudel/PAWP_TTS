\section{Diagnostic Weight-Space Programming: Mechanisms and Confounders}
\label{sec:wsp}

Zero-training, activation-guided weight edits were evaluated systematically on \texttt{PAWP-1.5M} under strict controls: hyperparameter selection on \texttt{val.jsonl} only, one-shot verification on untouched $300+300$ \texttt{test.jsonl}, paired-bootstrap 95\% CIs, matched-norm random edits, and same-parameter gradient fine-tuning controls. Three experiments revealed both genuine mechanistic properties and critical methodological pitfalls; Figure~\ref{fig:forest} summarizes every edit against its controls. Because several edits and controls were tested and $p$-values are not corrected for multiple comparisons, all results in this section are exploratory. Edits are ROME-style~\cite{meng2022rome} rank-1 updates. For a linear map $W$, let $\bar h$ be the mean input activation over validation frames of the target class and $C$ the input covariance over non-target frames. The key direction is $\hat k = C_\lambda^{-1}\bar h / \lVert C_\lambda^{-1}\bar h\rVert$ with $C_\lambda = C + \lambda I$, so the edit responds weakly to non-target inputs, and the update is $\Delta W = \alpha\,\lVert W\rVert_F\,\hat u\,\hat k^\top$, where $\hat u$ is the unit output direction to be strengthened and the scale $\alpha$ is swept on validation only. Random controls use Gaussian unit vectors $\hat u_r,\hat k_r$ at the same location with the same $\alpha\lVert W\rVert_F$. Gradient controls fine-tune exactly the same parameters with Adam, with learning rate and step count also selected on validation.

\subsection{Unconstrained Shared Edits vs.\ Language-Residual Isolation}
In \texttt{EXP-013a}, an unconstrained Rank-1 edit in shared \texttt{acoustic\_blocks.3.pw2.weight} reduced target confusion but degraded overall PER (NE $+1.22\%$, EN $+1.75\%$). Furthermore, grapheme auditing revealed that 90.3\% (65/72) of the targeted \texttt{/b/ $\rightarrow$ /w/} errors were a \texttt{v2} cache-label artifact on \textit{va} words. Rebuilding \texttt{v3} eliminated the artifact entirely, underscoring that mechanistic editing must never be applied before label consistency is verified.

\subsection{Word-Boundary Pause Selectivity and Global Duration Bias (\texttt{EXP-016})}
On clean \texttt{v3} validation data, word-boundary \texttt{<sp>} tokens were under-predicted ($6.16$ vs.\ $10.38$ frames) and 90.8\% linearly separable from regular phonemes at the 96-dim input of \texttt{duration\_predictor.proj.weight} (96 params = 0.0064\% of model). A ROME-whitened Rank-1 update achieved \textbf{27:1 selectivity} on untouched test data (\texttt{<sp>} $+1.001$ frames vs.\ phonemes $+0.037$ frames), reduced deletions ($-19$ NE, $-21$ EN), and beat dev-tuned 96-param gradient FT (NE PER 4.93\% vs.\ 5.55\%; Figure~\ref{fig:forest}).

However, the edit did not beat a matched random-96 control on overall NE PER (4.93\%, $p=0.177$ vs.\ 4.84\%, $p=0.004$). A per-class duration audit (all 169 Nepali utterances of the validation split, 13,450 phonemes; the PER sweep below uses a fixed 150+150 subset) found the cause: baseline durations are \textbf{globally biased short by $-7.3\%$} across every phoneme class (pred $6.105$f vs.\ GT $6.585$f; ratio $0.927$ overall, ranging from $0.869$ to $0.965$), consistent with Jensen's inequality under log-duration training. Selecting a 1-parameter global duration bias shift (\texttt{duration\_predictor.proj.bias}) strictly on \texttt{val.jsonl} ($n=150$ NE + $150$ EN; selected $\Delta b = +0.10$, improving average validation PER from 4.625\% to 4.448\%) and evaluating \textbf{once} on untouched \texttt{test.jsonl} ($n=300$ NE + $300$ EN) reduced corpus-level Nepali PER from 4.744\% ($D=401$) to \textbf{4.513\%} ($D=373$, $-0.23$\,pp); the mean paired per-utterance change is $-0.27$\,pp (95\% CI $[-0.41, -0.12]$, $p<0.001$), with no significant English change (4.044\% $\rightarrow$ 4.016\%, $p=0.317$). This audit used its own synthesis and scoring pass, whose unedited baseline (4.744\% NE, 4.044\% EN) differs from the Table~\ref{tab:params} pass (5.04\% / 4.02\%) by up to 0.29\,pp. All deltas in this section and in Figure~\ref{fig:forest} are therefore paired within a single pass, and the pass-to-pass variation is itself of the same order as the effects studied. Global speaking-rate calibration thus explains the random-96 gain. Because the predictor outputs linear frames but is trained with a log-domain loss, a $+0.10$ pre-softplus shift adds only $\approx$0.1 frame per phoneme and \emph{partially} corrects the $-7.3\%$ bias; the proper fix is a bias-corrected duration loss at training time.

\subsection{Target Validity Against Native Human Recordings (\texttt{EXP-017})}
After \texttt{v3} re-alignment, the top remaining Nepali substitution on validation was retroflex nasal to dental nasal (IPA retroflex nasal $\rightarrow$ dental /n/). Applying a ROME null-space Rank-1 edit strictly inside the Nepali-only low-rank residual \texttt{encoder\_blocks.0.pw2.\{u\_ne, v\_ne\}} (6,144 params = 0.41\%) left English output unchanged (by construction, since the residual is gated off for English, so this is an architectural guarantee rather than a measured property of the edit) and changed overall Nepali PER by only 0.01\,pp (5.036\% $\rightarrow$ 5.026\%, $p=0.495$). The edit also failed on its target. With the validation-selected scale ($\alpha=0.01$, $\lVert\Delta W\rVert_F=0.259$), the target confusion rate on test was unchanged (41.07\%, $n=56$ occurrences), and the target logit margin moved by only $-0.009$ (95\% CI $[-0.028, +0.006]$, $p=0.148$). Same-parameter gradient fine-tuning moved the margin significantly more ($-0.041$, $p<0.001$), although it too left the confusion rate unchanged.

Critically, running \texttt{ctc\_aligner\_v3.pt} directly on \textbf{ground-truth human studio Nepali audio} in \texttt{test.jsonl} ($n=62$ occurrences across 52 utterances) decoded \textbf{90.3\% (56/62)} of orthographic retroflex-nasal occurrences as dental /n/ (mean margin $+3.246$). This measurement has a caveat: the recognizer was itself trained on frontend labels and may not discriminate the retroflex--dental nasal contrast reliably, and the reference inventory~\cite{khatiwada2009nepali} lists a retroflex nasal. Confirmation by native listeners is therefore pending. On the current evidence, \texttt{EXP-017}'s target is better explained as a frontend phonology choice (proposed as an unimplemented future \texttt{v4} frontend rule, as ~1,800 lexicon entries still emit the retroflex symbol), not a neural model defect.
